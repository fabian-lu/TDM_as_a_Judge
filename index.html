§ 1   The question

Judges that do not write

Large language models are increasingly used to judge the outputs of other models. In pairwise judging, the judge reads two answers to the same question and decides which is better. Such judges are known to be biased. Their verdicts can change with cues that should not matter, for example the order in which the answers appear or a claim that most people prefer one of them. The CALM benchmark collects twelve of these biases and finds that even strong LLM judges are swayed by several of them.

Typed decision models (TDMs) offer a more direct route. Given an input and one or more questions with a fixed set of options, a TDM returns a probability for each option in a single pass, without generating any text. TypeSafe, for example, presents its TDM Jev as faster and cheaper than LLMs, with calibrated probabilities, and TDMs are already used as judges. Most TDMs, however, are LLMs fine-tuned to return probabilities instead of text, so they may have inherited the biases of LLM judges. A systematic test of these biases on TDMs was missing.

We therefore apply CALM's method to TDMs. We run its tests, and three of our own, on eight TDMs and on seven LLM judges of 2024 and 2026, all with the same prompts. This lets us ask three questions. Are TDMs biased? Are they more or less biased than LLM judges? And what do their probabilities reveal that a verdict alone would hide?

§ 2   How we test

Change one thing that should not matter

Each test takes a question with a better and a worse answer, changes one thing that should not affect the verdict, and keeps everything else fixed. A fair judge gives the same verdict before and after. We use CALM's items, prompts and templates. Where CALM's released file had attached most fake citations to the wrong items, we generated them again as CALM describes.

A judge may also change a verdict by chance, so we send every unperturbed prompt twice. The excess counts how many more verdicts the cue changes than a plain repetition does. For example, a judge that changes 20 of 100 verdicts under a cue and 2 of 100 when the prompt is simply sent again has an excess of 18. A test counts as a bias when the whole 95% interval of the excess lies above 5 per 100.

excess  E = CR − RR  consistency rate on a repetition minus robustness rate under the cue
Why not the robustness rate aloneBy CALM's robustness rate, the median TDM would look more robust than the median 2026 LLM judge in eight of eleven tests, by the excess in only three. TDMs almost never change a verdict by chance, LLM judges sometimes do.
The excess is cautiousWhen a cue and chance both act on the same verdict, it flips back. The excess can therefore understate the effect of a cue, but never overstate it.

§ 3   What we found

Six findings

3.1Every TDM is biased

Each of the eight TDMs is biased in three (Solar) to eight (Kev) of the twelve tests. Sentiment and position bias all eight, sentiment most strongly, while five tests bias at most one. Which test is run matters far more than which TDM is tested. In a mixed model of the probability shift on each prompt, the test explains 25% of the variance and the model only 1.4%.

How to read the fingerprintsEach glyph is one judge. Each petal is one test, always in the same place, and its size grows with the excess. A filled petal is a bias, an outlined one is unclear, a faint one is no bias. Select a judge to follow it in the chart below.

3.2More biased than the LLM judges of 2026

In bias, the TDMs lie between the two LLM judges of 2024 and above those of 2026. The newer LLM judges differ from the older ones mainly in how they treat the presentation of an answer. They hardly react to its position any more and punish padding, while their reactions to tone and citations have not become weaker. The TDMs, in contrast, still react strongly to position.

  • Position, bandwagon and compassion fade are biases in eight, five and three TDMs, but in no 2026 LLM judge.
  • Even Jev, with one of the smallest position biases among the TDMs (8), exceeds every 2026 LLM judge (at most 6). The two 2024 judges reach 37 and 21.
  • Sentiment and a fake citation on the better answer affect both kinds of judge alike.
  • Padding the better answer, in contrast, biases all five 2026 LLM judges but only three TDMs.

The position bias persists although every prompt tells the judge to ignore the order of the answers. It has to be removed by the design of the request, for example by asking in both orders.

A TDM can add a biasUnder bandwagon, Qwen3.8-27B changes 6 of 100 verdicts beyond chance. Perplexity Decider and Clef, two TDMs built on it, change 26 and 27.

3.3Tripped up by the same prompts

Despite these differences, the two kinds of judge flip on the same prompts. When a TDM changes its verdict on a prompt, a 2026 LLM judge changes its own on 27% of these prompts, against 10% of the others. That is as much overlap as between two TDMs. In a logistic mixed model, the prompt effects of the TDMs and of the 2026 LLM judges correlate at 0.95, and the prompt accounts for about 40% of the variation in flips.

Under sentiment, the prompt accounts for 90% of the variation in the flips of the LLM judges, so some rewrites flip almost every judge and others almost none. Part of what a test measures thus lies in its prompts. Either these items are hard for any model used as a judge, or their rewrites change more than the cue, and only human judgments of the same items could tell the two apart.

Similar models, similar profilesOver the twelve tests, the bias profiles of two TDMs correlate at 0.69 on average, those of two 2026 LLM judges at 0.77, and those of a TDM and a 2026 LLM judge at 0.62.

3.4Where a cue sits decides the harm

The TDMs punish fake citations, the reverse of the trust in citations that CALM found for its LLM judges. Among our LLM judges only GPT-3.5 Turbo still rewards them. Whether a punished cue harms, however, depends on where it sits. With the citation on the worse answer, the accuracy of the median TDM rises from 0.66 to 0.81, which looks like robustness. With the citation on the better answer, it falls to 0.41. The rise is no merit, since the verdict then follows the citation rather than the quality of the answers.

CALM places most cues on the worse answer, where a punished cue looks harmless. This is why we added tests with the cue on the better answer. Benchmarks should place each cue where it can do harm, or on both answers.

PaddingPadding the better answer lowers the accuracy of the median 2026 LLM judge from 0.97 to 0.85 and that of the median TDM from 0.99 to 0.94. GPT-3.5 Turbo, which likes long answers, rises from 0.86 to 0.95.

3.5The probabilities show more than the verdicts

Because a TDM returns a probability, we can see how far a cue moves it, even when the verdict stays. Under authority, position, bandwagon and sentiment, the median TDM keeps its verdict while its probability moves by more than 0.10 on 19 to 24% of the prompts, about as often as the verdict flips. Such hidden shifts matter wherever the probability is used as a score, for example as a reward in training, since a cue that never flips a verdict still moves the score.

Most cues tip close calls. Outside sentiment and authority (on better), 30% of the verdicts given with a probability below 0.6 flip, against 3% of those given with more than 0.9. Each further 0.1 of confidence halves the odds of a flip. A tone or a citation on the better answer, however, flips even confident verdicts. A TDM's own probability is therefore a useful warning sign, except for exactly these two cues.

Calibration does not protectThe expected calibration error of the TDMs ranges from 0.07 (D1) to 0.25 (Solar), whose verdicts are 90% sure on average but right in only 67% of cases. Better calibration does not go with less bias.

3.6Cheap and consistent

On the original prompts, the TDMs are about as accurate as the 2026 LLM judges, slightly less so on the alignment set. They are far cheaper and faster. At the median, a 2026 LLM judge costs 37 times as much as a TDM and takes six times as long, mostly for the explanation of 90 to 540 tokens that CALM's prompt asks for. The TDMs are also more consistent: six of the eight repeat more than 99.7% of their verdicts when a prompt is sent again.

The low price does not buy freedom from bias. It does, however, make the checks cheap. A full bias test of one TDM cost between US$0.26 and US$3.11 in October 2026.

Asking twiceAsking a TDM in both orders and averaging its two probabilities removes the position bias by construction. It doubles the cost and changed accuracy by −0.2 to +3.8 points.

§ 5   Beyond the eight pages

From the appendix

The appendix of the paper holds much that did not fit into the main text. These are the parts we find most interesting.

The louder the majority, the more TDMs follow it

Every TDM is swayed more by a claimed majority of 90% than by one of 60%. The median 2026 LLM judge hardly reacts, with a robustness rate of 0.93 at 60% and 0.89 at 90%.

Calibration and bias are unrelated

Across the eight TDMs, the rank correlations of the calibration error and the Brier score with the mean excess lie between −0.36 and 0.40 and change sign between the two datasets. A well calibrated TDM is not a fair one.

Moving text into fields changes verdicts

When the same text moves from CALM's prompt into the separate fields of the vendor's native format, 0.8% (Jev) to 10.4% (Solar) of the verdicts change. Asking one question per criterion lowers the odds of a flip at the same confidence by 21%, but also makes the TDMs less sure, so about as many verdicts flip overall.

Asking in both orders

Averaging the probabilities of both orders removes the position bias, since each answer sits once in each place. Accuracy changed by −0.2 (Kev) to +3.8 points (Tev1), and only Tev1's gain has an interval that excludes zero.

Identity notes matter no more than a neutral note

Notes on the identity of an answer's author change no more verdicts than our neutral note (median excess 3 and 4), and the six identity groups do not differ from each other. CALM's judges had reacted most to female and refugee identities.

One interface, quite different systems

On a 50/50 answer, three TDMs always return the first option, and their calibration differs widely. A confidence of 0.8 does not mean the same for two models, so a threshold tuned on one cannot be carried over to another. No TDM is the least biased in every test, so the best model depends on the bias that matters for the task.

A larger excess is not always a larger bias

Under position, Clef's excess is larger than that of its base LLM Qwen3.8-27B (14 against 6), but only because Qwen also changes verdicts by chance. Under the swap itself, both change about as many verdicts (13.9 and 14.8%).

Close architecture, different behaviour

Tev1, which keeps the language-model head of its LLM and answers with one letter, has the bias profile least like that of the 2026 LLM judges (rank correlation 0.38). Jev (0.87), Perplexity (0.81) and Clef (0.80) are the most similar. With eight models and twelve tests, this is only a first indication.

§ 6   In practice

Replacing an LLM judge with a TDM?

A TDM is a good choice where cost, speed and consistency matter, and for tone, citations and padding a switch adds no bias. An LLM judge remains the safer choice where the inputs carry claimed majorities, popularity counts or the names of the systems that wrote the answers, where a verdict must be explained, and for judgments we have not tested, such as checking a derivation or reading long inputs. For a switch, the paper recommends:

  1. Ask in both orders and average the two probabilities. Every TDM we tested has a position bias.
  2. Remove cues the task does not need, such as claimed majorities, popularity counts and model names.
  3. Do not expect a TDM to handle tone and citations better. Both kinds of judge punish them even on the better answer.
  4. Treat close calls as uncertain and pass them to a second judge. Outside sentiment and authority (on better), three in five flipped verdicts come from verdicts given with a probability below 0.7.
  5. Use the probabilities with care. Check their calibration on the task's own data before using them as a score.
  6. Choose the model and the format for the task, and keep one format fixed.
  7. Read the interface. Check what the model returns on a 50/50 answer and how it defines its confidence.
  8. Test again after an update. TDMs are new and change quickly, and a full test costs a few dollars.

Test your own judge

We release all 14,223 prompts and every answer of the fifteen judges, with their probabilities, full replies, costs and timings. The prompts can be sent to any judge that compares two answers, and no code from the repository is needed to use them.

prompts = pd.read_json("data/prompts.jsonl.gz", lines=True)
jev = pd.read_json("data/answers/tdms/typesafe_jev-1.13__calm.jsonl.gz", lines=True)
d = jev.query("repeat == 0").merge(prompts, on="id")

Every perturbed prompt points to its unperturbed version, and r1_label says where the better answer is shown. A verdict is right if it picks that answer, and it flips if it differs from the verdict on the original. To rerun the analysis or send the prompts to another TDM or LLM judge, see the code.

§ 8   Limitations

What we cannot say

All our tests are pairwise comparisons with a reference, a correct answer or a dataset label, so judges that score single answers or stand in for a human's perception remain untested. We rely on CALM's items and rewrites, which GPT-4-Turbo wrote and which may change more than the intended property. Our citations are fake, as in CALM, so whether punishing them is a bias or sound judgment needs a test with real sources.

The flips we report describe how a judge's output changes with its input, not why. The training data of the models are not public, so we cannot attribute differences between models to their size or training. Finally, TDMs are new, and our results describe the models of October 2026. The tests should be repeated on later models, which the released code and data allow for a few dollars per model.
